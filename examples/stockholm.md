# Stockholm: a history in six chapters

Stockholm began as a fortified islet guarding the narrow passage between a great freshwater lake and the Baltic Sea. Over nearly eight centuries it grew into a royal capital, an industrial city, and finally one of the most connected places in Europe. These six chapters follow that story from the first timber walls to the glass towers of today.

## The founding of a city on the water

Stockholm owes its existence to geography. At the eastern end of Lake Mälaren, the water drains into the Baltic through a handful of narrow channels, and in the middle of them sits a small rocky island that Swedes now call Stadsholmen. Whoever held that island could watch every ship that moved between the sea and the rich farming districts around the lake. In the thirteenth century, that was reason enough to build a fortress there.

The man credited with founding the city is Birger Jarl, the powerful regent who ruled Sweden on behalf of his young son, King Valdemar. The oldest surviving documents that mention Stockholm by name are two letters from 1252, and historians usually place the founding in the years around 1250. Nobody knows the exact date, however, and archaeologists have found traces suggesting that people used the island, and perhaps fortified it, somewhat earlier. The year 1252 marks the first written record, not necessarily the first stone.

Tradition offers a more dramatic origin story. In 1187, raiders from across the Baltic sacked Sigtuna, then one of the leading towns on Lake Mälaren, and according to the old account the survivors decided to protect the lake by blocking its entrance. A later legend claims they floated a log down the water and built their new town wherever it washed ashore. The name itself is just as uncertain. The word "holm" means a small island, and "stock" may refer to a log, a pile driven into the lakebed, or a timber barrier, but historians disagree about which explanation is right, and none can be proven.

What is clear is that the new town grew quickly. The land around Stockholm has been slowly rising since the end of the last Ice Age, as the earth rebounds from the weight of the vanished glaciers. By the 1200s this uplift had made older waterways shallower, which pushed more and more traffic through the channels beside Stadsholmen. A castle with a strong central tower rose on the island, and a cluster of merchant houses, warehouses, and wooden quays spread out around it.

Trade gave the town its character. Iron and copper from the mining districts of central Sweden flowed down to Stockholm, and from there ships carried the metal out to the Baltic ports. Much of this commerce ran through the hands of German merchants, especially those connected to Lübeck and the network of trading cities later known as the Hanseatic League. Germans settled in Stockholm in large numbers, and for a long time the town law required that half of the city council be German.

The church followed the merchants. The great town church, dedicated to Saint Nicholas and known today as Storkyrkan, stood near the highest point of the island, and a Franciscan friary was founded on the neighboring islet that would later be called Riddarholmen. Magnus Ladulås, Birger Jarl's son, was buried in the friary church in 1290, beginning its long role as a royal burial place. By the end of the century, Stockholm had become more than a fortress. It was a town with a market, a council, and ambitions.

The medieval town was cramped and crowded, built on a scrap of rock barely large enough to hold it. Narrow lanes ran down to the water on every side, and buildings were mostly wooden, which made fire a constant danger. Yet the street pattern laid down in these first generations still survives in Gamla stan, the old town, where visitors today walk the same crooked alleys that medieval merchants once crowded with barrels and bales.

## Merchants, unions, and a bloodbath

The fourteenth and fifteenth centuries made Stockholm the most valuable prize in Sweden, and that value turned it into a battleground. The city controlled the trade of the Mälaren region, held the strongest castle in the kingdom, and could be supplied by sea. Kings, regents, and foreign princes all understood that whoever held Stockholm held a large part of Sweden.

The city's German ties shaped its politics during these years. In 1364 a group of Swedish nobles invited Albert of Mecklenburg, a German prince, to take the Swedish throne, and Stockholm's German burghers became some of his firmest supporters. When Albert lost his war against Queen Margaret of Denmark in 1389, Stockholm held out for him for years. Pirates known as the Victual Brothers ran supplies into the besieged city, and they earned their name by bringing in food when nothing else could get through.

Margaret's victory led to the Kalmar Union of 1397, which joined Denmark, Norway, and Sweden under a single monarch. On paper the union was a partnership, but many Swedes came to see it as Danish rule, and Stockholm sat at the center of the struggle. Over the following century the city changed hands repeatedly between union kings and Swedish regents. Its walls, its castle, and its loyalty were constantly in play.

One of the turning points came in 1471. That autumn the Swedish regent Sten Sture the Elder met the Danish king Christian I at the Battle of Brunkeberg, on a ridge just north of the old town in what is now the busy district of Norrmalm. Sture won, and the victory became a symbol of Swedish independence. The same year, the rule requiring a German half of the city council was abolished, a sign that Stockholm was becoming a Swedish town in politics as well as geography.

Sture also left a lasting monument. In 1489 a magnificent wooden sculpture of Saint George slaying the dragon, carved by the Lübeck master Bernt Notke, was unveiled in Storkyrkan. It was widely understood as a celebration of Brunkeberg, with the saint standing for Sweden and the dragon for its enemies. The sculpture still stands in the church today, one of the finest works of late medieval art in northern Europe.

The union's final crisis produced the darkest episode in the city's history. In 1520 the Danish king Christian II invaded Sweden, and after the young regent Sten Sture the Younger was mortally wounded, his widow Christina Gyllenstierna led the defense of Stockholm for months. She finally surrendered in exchange for promises of amnesty. Christian was crowned in Storkyrkan on November 4, 1520, and for several days the city celebrated.

Then the king turned on his former enemies. On November 7 a church tribunal declared a number of Sture supporters guilty of heresy, and on November 8 the executions began in Stortorget, the main square of the old town. Bishops, nobles, burghers, and townspeople were beheaded or hanged over two days, and most accounts put the number of dead at around eighty or more. The Stockholm Bloodbath, as it came to be called, was meant to crush resistance. It had the opposite effect.

Among the nobles whose families suffered was a young man named Gustav Eriksson Vasa, whose father was executed in the square. Gustav raised a rebellion in the province of Dalarna, and over the next two years his forces drove the Danes back. On June 6, 1523, he was elected king at Strängnäs, a date Sweden now celebrates as its national day. Stockholm surrendered that month, and at midsummer Gustav made his entry into the city that had witnessed his family's tragedy. The Kalmar Union was finished, and a new Swedish kingdom had begun.

Gustav Vasa's reign transformed Stockholm in quieter ways too. He broke the power of the Catholic Church, confiscated its property, and supported the reformer Olaus Petri, who preached in Storkyrkan. A famous painting of a halo phenomenon seen over the city in 1535, known as the Sun Dog Painting, survives in a later copy and offers one of the oldest known images of Stockholm. It shows a small walled town crowded onto its island, about to become the heart of a far larger state.

## The age of greatness

In the seventeenth century, Sweden briefly became one of the great powers of Europe, and Stockholm grew to match its new role. Swedes call this period stormaktstiden, the age of great power. It began with the reign of Gustavus Adolphus, who ruled from 1611 to 1632, and it lasted until the early 1700s. During those decades the city was transformed from a modest trading town into a capital with palaces, broad streets, and international ambitions.

Gustavus Adolphus and his chancellor Axel Oxenstierna built the machinery of a modern state. Government offices, courts, and colleges gathered in Stockholm, and the Instrument of Government of 1634 formally confirmed the city as the capital of the realm. Officials, soldiers, craftsmen, and servants poured in to staff this growing apparatus. Most estimates suggest the population rose from around ten thousand people early in the century to roughly fifty or sixty thousand by its end.

The most famous symbol of these ambitions sank in full view of the city. On August 10, 1628, the warship Vasa set out on her maiden voyage from the harbor below the castle, heavily armed and lavishly decorated with carved sculptures. She sailed only a little over a kilometer before a gust of wind heeled her over, water poured through the open gun ports, and she went down with the loss of at least thirty people. The ship was too tall and too lightly ballasted for her heavy upper decks. Remarkably, she lay preserved in the cold, brackish water until she was raised in 1961, and today she stands in her own museum on Djurgården.

The city outgrew its island during these decades. Planners laid out straight streets in a grid on Norrmalm to the north and Södermalm to the south, replacing irregular clusters of shacks with orderly blocks. Merchants and nobles built stone houses, and the old town itself was partly rebuilt in more durable materials. The House of Nobility, Riddarhuset, rose beside the water as a meeting place for the aristocracy, one of the most elegant buildings of the era.

Stockholm also became a center of European culture and finance. Queen Christina, who came to the throne as a child and ruled in her own right from 1644 until her abdication in 1654, gathered scholars at her court. The French philosopher René Descartes came to Stockholm at her invitation and died there in the winter of 1650. A few years later, in 1661, Johan Palmstruch's Stockholm Banco issued the first banknotes in Europe, and in 1668 the parliament founded the bank now known as Sveriges Riksbank, the oldest central bank still in operation.

Architecture reached new heights through a remarkable family. Nicodemus Tessin the Elder designed Drottningholm Palace on an island in Lake Mälaren, begun in 1662, which remains the private residence of the royal family. His son, Nicodemus Tessin the Younger, trained in Rome and Paris and brought the grand Baroque style to Sweden. Father and son together shaped the look of the capital for generations.

The younger Tessin's greatest commission came out of disaster. On May 7, 1697, fire broke out in the old Tre Kronor castle, the medieval fortress that had been rebuilt and expanded over the centuries. The body of King Charles XI, who had died only weeks earlier, lay in state inside, and it was carried out as the building burned. Within hours most of the castle was gone, along with a large part of the royal archives and library.

Tessin had already been working on a new design for the castle, and after the fire he drew up plans for an entirely new palace on the same site. The result was the Royal Palace that dominates the old town today, a vast Italianate block built around a central courtyard. Construction was slow, interrupted by war and lack of money, and the royal family did not move in until 1754. By then the age of greatness was over, but the palace remained its most enduring monument.

## Plague, enlightenment, and industry

The eighteenth century opened with catastrophe. Sweden was fighting the Great Northern War against Russia and its allies, and in 1710 plague arrived in Stockholm. Over the following months it killed about a third of the population, perhaps twenty thousand people, emptying whole streets and overwhelming the parish graveyards. The war ended in 1721 with Sweden's empire largely lost, and the capital had to rebuild both its population and its confidence.

What followed was a period of political experiment. During the Age of Liberty, from 1719 to 1772, power shifted from the king to the parliament, where rival parties nicknamed the Hats and the Caps fought over policy. Stockholm became a lively place of newspapers, coffeehouses, and scientific debate. In 1739 a group of scholars including the botanist Carl Linnaeus founded the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences in the city, and Swedish science entered a golden age.

In 1772 the young King Gustav III ended the Age of Liberty with a bloodless coup and took power back into royal hands. He loved the theater, and under his patronage Stockholm gained an opera company, a new opera house, and in 1786 the Swedish Academy, which still guards the Swedish language and today awards the Nobel Prize in Literature. The elegant, restrained style of furniture and interiors from his reign is still known as Gustavian. The poet and songwriter Carl Michael Bellman captured the city's taverns, drinkers, and lovers in verses that Swedes sing to this day.

Gustav III's story ended in his own favorite building. At a masked ball in the opera house on March 16, 1792, a disaffected nobleman named Jacob Johan Anckarström shot him in the back. The king died nearly two weeks later. The assassination later inspired operas, most famously Verdi's A Masked Ball, and it marked the end of Stockholm's most theatrical era.

The early nineteenth century was a difficult time. Sweden lost Finland to Russia in 1809, and a French marshal, Jean-Baptiste Bernadotte, was chosen as heir to the throne and became King Charles XIV John in 1818. His descendants still occupy the throne. Stockholm itself stagnated. Its population grew slowly, its housing was poor, and its death rate was among the highest of any European capital.

Change arrived with industry and the railway. The first railway line into Stockholm opened in 1860, and the Central Station followed in 1871, connecting the capital to the rest of the country. Factories, mills, and workshops multiplied, and in 1876 Lars Magnus Ericsson opened a small telegraph repair shop that would grow into one of the world's major telecommunications companies. People streamed in from the countryside, and the population rose from under a hundred thousand in 1850 to around three hundred thousand by 1900.

Growth brought misery as well as wealth. Many newcomers crowded into wooden shacks and overcrowded tenements, especially on Södermalm, where sanitation was poor and disease was common. At the same time, the city began to plan for a modern future. The Lindhagen plan of 1866 laid out wide boulevards and esplanades such as Strandvägen and Sveavägen, and the old stone-built inner districts gradually took on the grand apartment blocks that define them today.

Politics modernized too. In 1866 the Riksdag replaced its ancient four estates of nobles, clergy, burghers, and peasants with a two-chamber parliament, a reform decided in Stockholm after fierce debate. The vote still favored property owners, and universal suffrage lay decades in the future, but the old society of estates was finished.

No one captured this restless city better than August Strindberg. His novel The Red Room, published in 1879, satirized Stockholm's journalists, officials, and bohemians, and it made him famous almost overnight. Strindberg lived at many addresses around the city and wrote about its streets, its islands, and its archipelago with a mixture of love and fury. By the time of his death in 1912, Stockholm had become the modern, ambitious capital his books had described.

## The modern capital takes shape

The twentieth century began with Stockholm showing itself to the world. In 1912 the city hosted the Olympic Games, with a new brick stadium built for the occasion that is still in use. The Games were among the first to use electronic timing and a public address system, and the American athlete Jim Thorpe won both the pentathlon and the decathlon. Stockholm proved it could organize an international event with efficiency and style.

The city's most famous modern building followed. Stockholm City Hall, designed by the architect Ragnar Östberg, was inaugurated on Midsummer Eve in 1923 on the shore of Lake Mälaren. With its tall tower, red brick walls, and golden mosaics, it blended national romanticism with older traditions. Every December the Nobel Prize banquet is held in its great hall, and the building has become a symbol of the city.

Stockholm soon embraced a very different style. The Stockholm Exhibition of 1930, led by the architect Gunnar Asplund, introduced functionalism to a wide Swedish public with its light, airy pavilions of glass and steel. The movement matched the ambitions of the Social Democrats, who came to power in 1932 and began building what they called the people's home, a welfare state that promised decent housing, health care, and security for all.

During the Second World War, Sweden stayed officially neutral, and Stockholm became a crossroads of diplomats, refugees, and spies. The government made concessions to Germany, including allowing troop transit by rail, which remains controversial. Yet the city also took in refugees from Finland, Norway, and the Baltic states, and in October 1943 almost all of Denmark's Jews escaped across the Øresund to safety in Sweden. The Stockholm diplomat Raoul Wallenberg went to Budapest in 1944 and helped save thousands of Hungarian Jews before disappearing into Soviet custody.

After the war, planners set out to rebuild the center of the city almost from scratch. The Norrmalm regulation, carried out from the 1950s to the 1970s, demolished large parts of the old Klara district, including hundreds of buildings, to make way for offices, shopping streets, and traffic. Five glass office towers rose at Hötorget, and Sergels torg became a sunken modern plaza with the Kulturhuset cultural center beside it. Many Stockholmers still mourn what was lost, and the destruction helped turn the public against large-scale demolition.

Protest eventually stopped the bulldozers. In May 1971 residents occupied Kungsträdgården to save a group of elm trees from being cut down for a new subway entrance, and after a tense standoff the city gave in. The so-called elm conflict became a landmark in Swedish urban activism, and planners after it showed more respect for the historic fabric of the city.

Beneath the streets, a different kind of construction was transforming daily life. The first line of the Stockholm subway, the tunnelbana, opened in 1950, and the network soon spread out to new suburbs. Many of its stations were later decorated by artists, and the system is often called the longest art gallery in the world. Planned suburbs such as Vällingby, built in the 1950s, were designed around their subway stations with shops, services, and housing close at hand.

The housing shortage drove the most ambitious program of all. Between 1965 and 1974 the national Million Program aimed to build a million new homes across Sweden, and around Stockholm it produced large estates of apartment blocks in suburbs such as Skärholmen, Tensta, Rinkeby, and Husby. The program ended the worst shortages and gave many families modern kitchens and bathrooms for the first time. Over the following decades, however, some of these suburbs came to face problems of segregation and unemployment that remain a political issue today.

The decade also gave the world a phrase. In August 1973 a bank robbery at Norrmalmstorg in central Stockholm turned into a six-day hostage drama, during which the captives seemed to side with their captors against the police. The psychiatrist Nils Bejerot is usually credited with the term Stockholm syndrome to describe this bond, and the expression spread around the world, far beyond the square where it began.

## Stockholm in a connected century

On the night of February 28, 1986, Prime Minister Olof Palme was walking home from a cinema with his wife Lisbet, without bodyguards, when a gunman shot him on Sveavägen in central Stockholm. He died almost at once, and the killer escaped into the dark side streets. A man convicted of the murder in 1989 was acquitted on appeal, and the investigation dragged on for decades. In 2020 prosecutors named a deceased suspect and closed the case, but many Swedes remain unconvinced, and the killing is still remembered as the moment the country lost some of its innocence.

The economy that followed looked very different from the industrial city of a century earlier. Ericsson had made Stockholm a center of telecommunications, and in the 1990s the district of Kista, north of the city, grew into a hub for mobile and computer technology. The arrival of broadband and mobile internet gave Swedish companies a head start in the digital economy. Stockholm became known as a city where new technology was adopted early and tried out by ordinary people.

Startups were the next chapter. Skype, founded in 2003 by the Swede Niklas Zennström and the Dane Janus Friis with a team of Estonian engineers, showed how a small Nordic company could change global communication. Spotify, founded in Stockholm in 2006 by Daniel Ek and Martin Lorentzon, reshaped how the world listens to music. Companies such as the payments firm Klarna and the game studios behind Minecraft and Candy Crush followed, and by the 2010s Stockholm was often described as one of Europe's leading startup cities.

The city has also faced violence in recent years. On April 7, 2017, a man drove a stolen truck down Drottninggatan, a busy pedestrian shopping street, and crashed into a department store. Five people were killed and many more injured. The perpetrator, who had sympathized with the Islamic State group, was convicted of terrorism and sentenced to life imprisonment. In the days after the attack, people covered the area with flowers, and the city's response was marked more by grief and solidarity than by panic.

Growth has continued at a remarkable pace. The municipality is home to close to a million people, and the wider county to well over two million. New districts have risen on former industrial land, among them Hammarby Sjöstad, built around a lake that was once lined with factories, and the Royal Seaport on the old gasworks and harbor sites. Projects such as a new commuter rail tunnel under the city center, opened in 2017, and the long rebuilding of the Slussen junction have tried to keep the infrastructure in step with the population.

Climate and the environment have become central to how the city sees itself. Stockholm introduced a congestion charge for cars entering the center, first as a trial in 2006 and then permanently, and in 2010 it became the first city to be named European Green Capital by the European Commission. The water around the city, once polluted by sewage and industry, is now clean enough that people swim in the middle of the capital on summer days. Rising sea levels and heavier rainfall are new challenges, but the land uplift that shaped Stockholm's founding still continues, slowly lifting the city out of the water.

Beyond the city lies the archipelago, tens of thousands of islands, skerries, and rocks stretching out toward the open Baltic. For generations, Stockholmers have taken the white steamboats and ferries out to summer cottages, fishing villages, and bathing rocks. The archipelago shaped Strindberg's writing and countless family memories, and it remains the place where many residents feel the city's deepest connection to the sea.

Nearly eight centuries after Birger Jarl's time, Stockholm is still defined by water. Its fourteen central islands are linked by more than fifty bridges, and the channel where Lake Mälaren meets the Baltic still runs past the old town. The fortress that once guarded that passage is gone, replaced by a palace, a parliament, and a city of glass and brick. Yet the reason the city exists has not changed: it stands where the lake meets the sea.
